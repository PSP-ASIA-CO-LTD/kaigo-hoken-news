*Last updated: 11 Oct 2026*

Thai community long-term care has no single provider. A dependent older person at home may see a care manager from the local health centre, a caregiver from the village, a village health volunteer, and, in some districts, a separate community caregiver paid by the Department of Older Persons. The money comes through the local government. This guide sets out who each of them is, how they are trained and paid, and which records they keep. The step-by-step process is in [The NHSO long-term care cycle]({{< relref "/thailand/nhso-ltc-cycle" >}}).

## Care managers

**Who they are.** A care manager, ผู้จัดการการดูแลผู้สูงอายุ (phu chatkan kan dulae phu sung-ayu, care manager), finds, assesses and plans care for dependent people, and makes sure they can reach the health services they need. Care managers link up with district and provincial managers.[^1] The post was added to the health system without new staff. People already in post take it on as extra work.[^1] Most are registered nurses at sub-district health promoting hospitals. In some areas physiotherapists, Thai traditional medicine practitioners or public-health officers do it. A few come from local governments, mostly large ones with their own health division.[^1] A second fact sheet says that in Thailand all care managers come from public-health jobs, mostly nursing, whereas Japan uses social workers.[^3] It adds that each primary-care facility usually has at least one care manager for the dependent people in its area, and that some areas are left without one when a care manager moves and no new course has opened.[^3]

**Training and qualifications.** A care manager must pass the Department of Health's 70-hour care-manager course, run by the regional health centre, ศูนย์อนามัย (sun anamai, regional health centre), for their own area.[^1] A 2016 municipal fund's rules give fuller qualifications: aged at least 25; a bachelor's degree in medicine, nursing or public health (or a nursing and midwifery certificate) with three years' experience, or another degree, practical-nurse or technical-nurse training with three years' experience with older people; and the 70-hour course, from the Department of Health or another body using its curriculum or an equivalent.[^7] We did not find a current national rule with the same detail.

**What they do.** The care-manager fact sheet lists these duties:[^1]

- plan each person's care (the care plan), covering home visits and the health services each person needs, and coordinate the other professions in the area
- support caregivers' learning and supervise their work against the plan
- enter the care plan and the caregivers' work in the Department of Health's database, and enter each person's details in the NHSO's LTC database to claim the money

**Ratio.** The NHSO fund sets a target of one care manager for every 5 to 10 caregivers.[^1] A 2021 study counted:[^1]

| Item | Number |
|---|---|
| Care managers in the NHSO LTC fund | 13,615 |
| Caregivers in the LTC fund, 2020 (Department of Health data) | 86,829 |
| Ratio, care managers to caregivers | 1 : 6.4 |
| Local care volunteers working as caregivers | 13,190 |
| Ratio including them | 1 : 7.3 |

Source for the table: HRDO fact sheet on care managers.[^1] The authors warn that the target ratio assumes care managers have no other job, which is not the case in Thailand.[^1]

**Pay.** Care managers are paid from the LTC fund's per-person budget, but there is no fixed rate, unlike caregivers.[^1] A 2016 rate annex capped the care manager's yearly assessment and plan at 800 baht per person.[^7]

**Known problems.** The 2021 study lists five:[^1]

- care managers are health staff, but the money is run by local governments under local-government spending rules that health staff do not know
- care managers often move or change jobs, and regional health centres lack the budget to train replacements
- it is extra work on top of a full job, and no one else can do it without the course
- the Department of Health's care-plan system and the NHSO's system are not linked, so care managers do the same paperwork twice
- most care plans are written without advice from other professions

Some areas train local-government staff to take the paperwork and payment side, while the health-side care manager does the plan. The study found this in Sukhothai, Chon Buri, Songkhla and Nakhon Pathom. Kalasin trains "mini CMs" locally.[^1]

### Who approves the care plan

The care plan is approved by the local LTC subcommittee, คณะอนุกรรมการสนับสนุนการจัดบริการดูแลระยะยาวสำหรับผู้สูงอายุที่มีภาวะพึ่งพิง (khana anukammakan sanapsanun kan chat borikan dulae raya yao, LTC subcommittee). A 2019 NHSO board notice says the fund pays "according to projects approved by" this subcommittee.[^5] The care manager submits the plans printed from 3C to the subcommittee.[^2] In the NHSO's 2023 workflow chart, the plans go to the subcommittee as a project; it approves the project, and the local government records the approval in the NHSO LTC programme.[^15]

One fact sheet says instead that "the care plan must be presented to the Department of Health every time".[^3] That probably refers to writing it in the Department of Health's 3C program, but the sheet does not say so. We follow the Royal Gazette notice.

## Caregivers

**Who they are.** A caregiver, ผู้ช่วยเหลือดูแลผู้สูงอายุ (phu chuai luea dulae phu sung-ayu, caregiver), also called นักบริบาล (nak boriban), helps dependent people at home. In the LTC fund most caregivers are village health volunteers, because Thailand builds the scheme on volunteering.[^3] On 10 October 2026 Hfocus reported the Director-General of the Department of Health as saying that 135,591 caregivers look after 560,461 dependent people in the community, against 86,829 caregivers in 2020.[^16][^1] The 2026 figure is a news report of a spoken number. Most of the care they give is help with daily living: bathing, eating, dressing, toileting and moving about.[^3]

**Training.** To work in the LTC fund a caregiver must pass the Department of Health's caregiver course. The Office of Non-Formal and Informal Education, กศน. (Ko So No), helps local health facilities run the training.[^3] There are two courses, 70 hours and 420 hours. A 420-hour certificate can also be used to work for private employers.[^3] In 2016 the basic requirements were age 18 or over, primary education, and at least 70 hours on the Department of Health's course or an equivalent.[^7] For the courses themselves and the schools, see [Caregiver training and schools]({{< relref "/thailand/caregiver-training-and-schools" >}}).

**Registration.** Caregivers are registered in the Department of Health's 3C program, and the care manager enters their work.[^2][^1] For the 2025 stimulus project, the local government also uploads each caregiver's contract and training certificate to the NHSO LTC programme.[^6]

**Pay.** A 2021 study found the following rates:[^3]

| Type of caregiver | Pay |
|---|---|
| Red Cross volunteer | None |
| Existing village health volunteer working as a caregiver, through a service unit | 600 baht a month for 1 to 5 cases, 1,500 baht for 6 to 10 (some areas pool and share equally) |
| Caregiver at a local government's own elderly centre | Monthly rate set locally, no higher than the local minimum wage |
| "Mo Hom Suk" volunteers trained as physiotherapy aides (Kalasin) | 50 baht a case, up to 12 cases a month, from a rehabilitation fund |
| Full-time caregiver in the public system | 3,000 to 9,000 baht a month, paid by the local government from its own budget (not the LTC fund) |

Source for the table: HRDO fact sheet on caregivers.[^3] The study concludes that relying on volunteers is a main reason care is not continuous.[^3] Since July 2025 a stimulus project has paid hired caregivers up to 5,000 or 6,000 baht a month, depending on training, directly from the NHSO (see [The NHSO long-term care cycle]({{< relref "/thailand/nhso-ltc-cycle" >}})).[^6]

### Local care volunteers

In 2019 the Ministry of Interior told every local government to hire at least two full-time caregivers to support the volunteers. These posts are paid monthly: 5,000 baht with the 70-hour course, 6,000 baht with 50 more hours. The posts were to be filled by FY2020.[^3] The fact sheet calls them "volunteer caregivers", อาสาสมัครนักบริบาล (asasamak nak boriban).[^3] The 2025 NHSO guideline refers to the Ministry of Interior's regulation on local care volunteers, อาสาสมัครบริบาลท้องถิ่น (asasamak boriban thongthin), and applies it to the stimulus hires.[^6] In 2020 there were 13,190 of them.[^1]

## Village health volunteers (อสม.)

Village health volunteers, อาสาสมัครสาธารณสุขประจำหมู่บ้าน (asasamak satharanasuk pracham muban, abbreviated อสม.), are the oldest community health workforce in Thailand. They are supported by the Department of Health Service Support (DHSS), กรมสนับสนุนบริการสุขภาพ (Krom Sanapsanun Borikan Sukkhaphap, สบส.).

- **Pay.** DHSS announced that the stipend, ค่าป่วยการ (kha puai kan, stipend), at the new rate of 2,000 baht a month, would be paid for the April 2024 round on 15 May 2024, together with 6,000 baht of back pay.[^8]
- **App.** DHSS's 2022 manual for "Smart อสม." trains volunteers to use the Smart อสม. app to work and report. Its first function is the monthly อสม. 1 report, which replaces the paper form.[^9] See [Government systems]({{< relref "/thailand/competitors/government-systems" >}}).
- **Link to long-term care.** Most LTC-fund caregivers are village health volunteers who took the caregiver course.[^3] The NHSO designed the fund to use existing volunteers: village health volunteers, elderly-care volunteers and disability-care volunteers.[^4]

## Local governments

Local governments, องค์กรปกครองส่วนท้องถิ่น (ongkon pokkhrong suan thongthin, abbreviated อปท.), are the main mechanism of the LTC fund.[^4] The NHSO pays through each area's local health security fund, which the NHSO and the local government both fund. A local government has to join that fund first, and the scheme expects it to add its own resources and staff.[^4] About 6,800 of more than 7,700 local governments had joined, according to a 2021 study.[^4]

In the NHSO LTC programme, the local government:[^15]

- imports each project and its approved care plans, with each person's ADL score, care group and TAI
- records the LTC subcommittee's approval
- draws up the agreement and payment voucher, and transfers the money to the unit running the project

It can run the money in one of two ways. It can sign an agreement with a local health facility, usually a sub-district health promoting hospital, which then runs the whole service under Ministry of Public Health rules. Or it can set up its own community elderly quality-of-life development centre and run the fund itself, with more freedom on caregiver pay.[^3] The 2025 stimulus project was open to sub-district administrative organisations, municipalities, Bangkok and Pattaya.[^6]

## Sub-district health promoting hospitals (รพ.สต.)

The sub-district health promoting hospital, โรงพยาบาลส่งเสริมสุขภาพตำบล (rongphayaban songsoem sukkhaphap tambon, abbreviated รพ.สต.), is Thailand's front-line primary-care unit. It matters to long-term care in two ways. It is where most care managers work,[^1][^2] and it is often the unit the local government contracts to run the service.[^3]

These hospitals are being moved from the Ministry of Public Health, กระทรวงสาธารณสุข (Krasuang Satharanasuk), to provincial administrative organisations, องค์การบริหารส่วนจังหวัด (ongkan borihan suan changwat, abbreviated อบจ.):[^10][^11]

| Fiscal year | Transferred | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 2008–2022 | 84 | 0.8% of 9,877 |
| 2023 | 3,263 | to 49 provincial governments |
| 2024 | 932 | |
| 2025 | 256 | |
| 2026 | 22 | |
| Total to date | 4,559 (46.2%) | 63 of 76 provincial governments; 5,318 remain with the ministry. The rows above add up to 4,557 |
| 2027 (requested) | 629 | 47 provincial governments, with 4,568 staff |

Sources for the table: 101 PUB, February 2026, citing the Health Systems Research Institute (rows to "Total to date");[^10] Hfocus, December 2025 (2027 row).[^11] 101 PUB reports that only 16 provinces have transferred every one of their hospitals.[^10] Staff choose whether to move with their hospital,[^10] so a care manager's employer may now be a provincial government rather than the ministry. We found no source on how the transfer has affected care-manager numbers.

## Service units and the other recipients

The 2019 NHSO board notice says the fund's money for dependent people goes to one of three kinds of recipient, for services in the benefit package and under projects the LTC subcommittee approves:[^5]

- **community elderly quality-of-life development centres**, ศูนย์พัฒนาคุณภาพชีวิตผู้สูงอายุในชุมชน (sun phatthana khunnaphap chiwit phu sung-ayu nai chumchon), set up by the local government
- **service units**, หน่วยบริการ (nuai borikan)
- **service facilities**, สถานบริการ (sathan borikan)

A 2016 municipal fund's rules use the same three kinds of body: its service units, service facilities and its own elderly quality-of-life centre each survey older people, employ care managers, and send proposals to the subcommittee.[^7] In 2024 the NHSO said it hoped the higher lump sum would draw in more capable units, such as community hospices, สถานชีวาภิบาล (sathan chiwaphiban).[^14] The notice we opened does not itself define a service unit or a service facility.

## The NHSO and the Department of Health

- **The NHSO head office** pulls the programme data and processes payments; **NHSO regional offices** check payment conditions[^15] and check stimulus-project contracts.[^6]
- **The Department of Health** sets the care-manager and caregiver courses[^1][^3] and runs 3C, built with Maejo University.[^2] Its regional health centres train care managers.[^1]

## Families

The NHSO designed the fund so that families and communities are the main carers, rather than institutions.[^4] The 2024 board announcement describes the care as given by the family-doctor team and the caregiver under the care plan.[^14]

## DOP community caregivers

The Department of Older Persons (DOP), กรมกิจการผู้สูงอายุ (Krom Kitchakan Phu Sung-ayu), in the Ministry of Social Development and Human Security, runs a separate scheme. Its "caregivers protecting the rights of older persons", ผู้บริบาลคุ้มครองสิทธิผู้สูงอายุ (phu boriban khumkhrong sitthi phu sung-ayu), are not part of the NHSO fund.

- **Role.** They work in five areas: health (including dementia and fall prevention), social (access to rights and welfare such as the old-age allowance), economic (income and jobs), environment (safe housing) and technology (phones and apps).[^12]
- **Training and pay.** A 240-hour DOP course, and 10,000 baht a month.[^12]
- **Who can apply.** Aged 18 to 65, at least lower-secondary education, living in or near the area and endorsed by community leaders, with no other regular official pay, and able to use basic communication devices.[^12]
- **Recruitment.** Provincial social development offices recruit for specific local governments. In Songkhla, for FY2026, the office advertised 8 posts, two each in four local governments, with applications from 18 to 26 December 2025.[^13] DOP opened another national recruitment round in October 2026.[^12]
- **System.** According to our [Nirun profile]({{< relref "/thailand/competitors/nirun" >}}), they record their work in NSTDA's Nirun for Community app.

The brief for this guide used the abbreviation "บสส." for these caregivers, and our Nirun profile uses it too. The DOP and provincial pages we opened use only the full name.[^12][^13]

{{< analysis title="Tako-San's take" >}}
Tako-San's view: the person at the centre of Thai community care, the care manager, is usually a nurse at a small primary-care unit, doing care management as extra work, trained once on a 70-hour course, and entering data into two government systems that do not talk to each other. Around them are caregivers who are mostly volunteers with no system login, local-government staff who handle the money under different rules, and, in some districts, a second set of DOP caregivers with their own app. Two changes are reshaping this. The NHSO's stimulus project put thousands of paid caregivers onto monthly records, and nearly half the primary-care units have moved to provincial governments, so the employer, the budget holder and the software buyer may now be different bodies in different provinces. For any software product, the lesson is that the buyer, the user and the payer are rarely the same, and a tool that saves the care manager time without changing who pays is the realistic starting point.
{{< /analysis >}}

## Not verified

- **Details of the 3C program.** The Department of Health's 3C report could not be reopened while this guide was written, so only its basic workflow is used. How accounts are approved, and its survey of care managers in health region 8, are left out until they can be checked.
- **Current number of care managers and caregivers.** The care-manager count is from a 2021 study using 2020 data; the 2026 caregiver count is a news report of a spoken figure. The Department of Health's open dataset (see [Data and IT reporting]({{< relref "/thailand/data-and-it-reporting" >}})) was not reopened for this guide.
- **Number of village health volunteers** receiving the 2,000-baht stipend. The source we opened gives the rate and timing, not the count.
- **Legal definitions of "service unit" and "service facility".** These come from the National Health Security Act, which we did not open for this guide.
- **The "บสส." abbreviation** for DOP community caregivers. Not found in official sources.
- **Effect of the hospital transfers on care managers.** No source found.
- **Whether local care volunteer posts were filled** in every local government by FY2020.
