Japan does not give each nursing-home resident a fixed public budget. Money follows the person through Long-Term Care Insurance (介護保険), which started in April 2000. The insurer is the municipality. People aged 65 and over can qualify for any cause; people aged 40 to 64 only for designated age-related diseases. A certificate (要介護認定) places the person in 要支援1–2 or 要介護1–5. A care manager writes the care plan.

For care at home, insurance pays only up to a monthly unit cap that rises with the care level. The user pays 10, 20, or 30 percent. In a facility, payment is a per-day fee by care level, plus add-ons. Room and meals are largely outside insurance. One unit is worth 10.00 to 11.40 yen depending on the service and the region, not a flat 10 yen.

Providers bill the prefectural National Health Insurance federation (国保連) by the 10th of the next month. They do not bill the ministry. Fees are rewritten about every three years. The last full revision was fiscal 2024 (＋1.59 percent). Fiscal 2026 added a mid-cycle ＋2.03 percent, aimed at wages, before the regular revision in fiscal 2027.

The claim flow is large: reviewed care costs in fiscal 2025 were 12.22 trillion yen. As of 1 October 2024 there were 37,264 home-visit offices, mostly companies, and 8,621 special nursing homes, almost all run by social-welfare corporations. Software must be in Japanese, emit national claim files and LIFE submissions, and absorb fee changes quickly. No product licence was found that makes a foreign package legal to bill with. Conformance is the test. The opening is workflow — records, rosters, LIFE, and claim preparation — not a new payment rail. Language, format lock-in, and incumbents such as Kaipoke, Honobono, Wiseman, Canamic, and Carekarte are the barriers.
