Japan does not give each nursing-home resident a fixed public budget. Money follows the person through Long-Term Care Insurance ({{< ja "介護保険" "kaigo hoken" >}}), which started in April 2000.[^1] The insurer is the municipality. People aged 65 and over can qualify for any cause; people aged 40 to 64 only for designated age-related diseases. A certificate ({{< ja "要介護認定" "yōkaigo nintei" "care-need certification" >}}) places the person in {{< ja "要支援" "yō-shien" "support needed" >}}1–2 or {{< ja "要介護" "yō-kaigo" "care needed" >}}1–5. A care manager writes the care plan.

For care at home, insurance pays only up to a monthly unit cap that rises with the care level. The user pays 10, 20, or 30 percent. In a facility, payment is a per-day fee by care level, plus add-ons. Room and meals are largely outside insurance. One unit is worth 10.00 to 11.40 yen depending on the service and the region, not a flat 10 yen.[^2]

Providers bill the prefectural National Health Insurance federation ({{< ja "国保連" "kokuho-ren" >}}) by the 10th of the next month.[^3] They do not bill the ministry. Fees are rewritten about every three years. The last full revision was fiscal 2024 (＋1.59 percent). Fiscal 2026 added a mid-cycle ＋2.03 percent, aimed at wages, before the regular revision in fiscal 2027.[^4]

The claim flow is large: reviewed care costs in fiscal 2025 were 12.22 trillion yen.[^5] As of 1 October 2024 there were 37,264 home-visit offices, mostly companies, and 8,621 special nursing homes, almost all run by social-welfare corporations.[^6] Software must be in Japanese, emit national claim files and LIFE submissions, and absorb fee changes quickly. No product licence was found that makes a foreign package legal to bill with. Conformance is the test. The opening is workflow — records, rosters, LIFE, and claim preparation — not a new payment rail. Language, format lock-in, and incumbents such as Kaipoke, Honobono, Wiseman, Canamic, and Carekarte are the barriers.
