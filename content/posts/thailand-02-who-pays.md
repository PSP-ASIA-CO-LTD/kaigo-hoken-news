**In three lines**

- The NHSO pays 10,442 baht a year for each dependent person with an approved care plan, through local governments and service units.
- A 2025 stimulus project added NHSO-paid caregivers, hired by local governments at 5,000 or 6,000 baht a month.
- The old-age allowance is cash, not care, and private care homes are paid by residents and families.

## The NHSO community long-term care money

The main public money for care at home is the NHSO's community long-term care scheme, run inside the Universal Coverage fund since a 2016 government policy and delivered through local governments.[^1]

**Who qualifies.** People with a Barthel ADL score of 11 or less: 0 to 4 is the bed-bound group, {{< th key="klum-tit-tiang" >}}, and 5 to 11 the home-bound group, {{< th key="klum-tit-ban" >}}. In 2024 the NHSO board added people with moderate dementia and people at the end of life.[^1]

**How much.** The NHSO board raised the lump sum from 6,000 to 10,442 baht per dependent person per year, announced in June 2024, and expected to cover about 600,000 people instead of 320,000.[^1] The FY2024 fund notice pays it to the service unit or the local government that runs the local health security fund, according to the number of people with an approved individual care plan.[^2] The cabinet's FY2026 framework for "public-health services for dependent people in the community" was 5,514,367,800 baht, up 90.13% on FY2025.[^3] That is a framework figure, not the final allocation.

**How it flows.** The care plan goes to a local LTC subcommittee, {{< th key="khana-anukammakan-ltc" >}}, as a project. Once the approval is recorded in the NHSO programme, the NHSO transfers the lump sum to the local government, which signs an agreement with the service unit and pays it.[^4] The 2019 NHSO notice names three kinds of recipient: community elderly centres, service units and service facilities.[^5] We found no current rule that splits the 10,442 baht by care group; how a subcommittee divides it is not set in any source we opened (see [The NHSO long-term care cycle]({{< relref "/thailand/nhso-ltc-cycle" >}})).

## Local governments and the stimulus caregivers

A local government must belong to its area's local health security fund, {{< th key="kongthun-lak-prakan-sukkhaphap-nai-radap-thongthin-rue-phuenthi" >}}, before it can join the scheme, and is expected to add its own resources.[^6]

In June 2025 the cabinet approved an economic-stimulus project, run by the NHSO, to pay caregivers hired by local governments. The money is outside the normal fund, and local governments do not co-fund it. Pay is up to 5,000 baht a month with the Department of Health's 70-hour course, or 6,000 baht with more than 70 hours. The project ran from 7 July 2025 to 30 September 2026, with targets of 18,587 caregivers and 106,807 dependent people.[^7] On 10 October 2026 the NHSO programme site showed:[^8]

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Caregivers employed | 12,125 |
| Pay under the agreements | 738,696,000 baht |
| Local governments with agreements | 2,117 |
| Dependent people cared for | 106,377 |
| Caregivers local governments want to keep paying | 11,273, at 1,877 local governments |

What happens after the project is covered in Part 5.

## The old-age allowance

The old-age allowance, เบี้ยยังชีพผู้สูงอายุ (bia yang chip phu sung-ayu, old-age living allowance), is a monthly cash payment by local governments: 600 baht at 60 to 69, 700 at 70 to 79, 800 at 80 to 89, and 1,000 baht at 90 and over. Its budget was reported as 90,000 million baht in FY2024.[^9] It is not tied to care need, so it does not pass through any care workflow.

## State welfare centres

DOP runs 12 social welfare development centres for older persons.[^10] Its table for 28 August 2026 covers 11 of them: 1,620 target places, 1,547 residents in the welfare group, and 8,679 people on waiting lists.[^11] The DOP page does not say what residents pay.[^12] See [Public and private providers]({{< relref "/thailand/public-and-private-providers" >}}).

## Private pay

Licensed private care homes set their own fees. The standards regulation requires each home to display its services and prices and to sign a written service contract.[^13] We found no official national price data, and no NHSO payment to private homes for their residents; the lump sum goes to service units and local governments on approved care plans.[^2]

## Where the money meets software

The NHSO money depends on a sequence of records: an ADL screening, a care plan, a subcommittee approval, an agreement and payment, and twelve months of service records.[^4] Those steps run through government systems (Part 4). When a public body buys software, it uses the national procurement law; see [How Thai public bodies buy software]({{< relref "/thailand/how-public-bodies-buy-software" >}}).

{{< analysis title="Tako-San's take" >}}
Tako-San's view: the money that moves through a care workflow is the NHSO lump sum, about 5.5 billion baht in the FY2026 framework, paid per approved care plan to thousands of local governments and service units. It is project money for care, so a care-software vendor should not expect it to fund software directly. The stimulus caregivers show how fast a national rule can create a new paid workforce that must be registered, paid monthly and reported on, but the project was time-limited, so it is not yet a stable base. The private side is simpler: homes pay from residents' fees, and the law already requires them to keep price lists, contracts and care records.
