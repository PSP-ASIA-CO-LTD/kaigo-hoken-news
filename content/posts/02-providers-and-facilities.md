Japanese care is a menu of designated services, not a single "nursing home" product. A provider is an office or a facility that a prefecture or a municipality has designated under the Long-Term Care Insurance Act. Counts below are from the national Survey of Long-term Care Service Facilities and Offices as of **1 October 2024**, unless noted. An office that offers two services is counted twice.[^1] [^2]

## Home, day, and short stay

These are the services a care plan usually mixes. The user lives at home, or in a private residence that is not itself an insurance facility.

**Home-visit care ({{< ja "訪問介護" "hōmon kaigo" >}}).** A helper comes to the home for body care (bathing, toileting, eating, transfers) or living assistance (cleaning, shopping, cooking). **37,264** offices, up 1.0 percent on the year. This is the closest match to a Thai home-visit service. It is also the service under the most commercial and financial stress, as the budget post notes.

**Home-visit nursing ({{< ja "訪問看護" "hōmon kango" >}}).** A nurse visits. Stations numbered **18,042**, up 9.9 percent. Nursing is a different profession and a different fee table from helper care, even though both happen in the home.

**Home-visit bathing ({{< ja "訪問入浴介護" "hōmon nyūyoku kaigo" >}}):** 1,641 offices. A team brings a portable bath.

**Day care ({{< ja "通所介護" "tsūsho kaigo" >}}),** often called day service ({{< ja "デイサービス" "dei sābisu" >}}). The person is picked up, spends the day at a centre, and goes home. **24,585** offices, essentially flat on the year. A smaller, municipality-designated cousin, community-based day care ({{< ja "地域密着型通所介護" "chiiki micchaku-gata tsūsho kaigo" >}}), had **18,921** offices and was shrinking (down 1.2 percent). Day rehabilitation ({{< ja "通所リハビリテーション" "tsūsho rihabiritēshon" >}}) is a separate medical-side service: **8,030** offices.

**Short stay ({{< ja "短期入所生活介護" "tanki nyūsho seikatsu kaigo" >}}, {{< ja "ショートステイ" "shōto sutei" >}}).** A few nights in a facility so the family can rest, or after a hospital discharge. **11,933** offices for daily-life short stay.

**Regular visiting and on-call care ({{< ja "定期巡回・随時対応型訪問介護看護" "teiki junkei zuiji taiō-gata hōmon kaigo kango" >}}).** A combined visit-and-nursing service meant to cover 24 hours in a neighbourhood. Only **1,441** offices, but up 6.2 percent. It is one of the models MHLW talks about for cities that will still be ageing in 2040.

Other home-side services a software suite eventually meets: welfare-equipment rental ({{< ja "福祉用具貸与" "fukushi yōgu taiyo" >}}, 7,736 offices), equipment sales, home modification, and visiting rehabilitation. Equipment rental and sales are dominated by companies (about 95 percent of offices in the 2024 detailed tables).

## Places people live

**Special nursing home ({{< ja "特別養護老人ホーム" "tokubetsu yōgo rōjin hōmu" >}}, {{< ja "特養" "toku-yō" >}}),** legally a long-term care welfare facility ({{< ja "介護老人福祉施設" "kaigo rōjin fukushi shisetsu" >}}) when it has 30 or more beds. This is the classic public-insurance residential facility: heavy care, long stay, high occupancy, and a long waiting list in many cities. **8,621** facilities, **604,469** beds, average size about 70 beds, occupancy **94.5 percent** (residents at end of September over beds). Smaller homes of 29 beds or fewer are community-based ({{< ja "地域密着型介護老人福祉施設" "chiiki micchaku-gata kaigo rōjin fukushi shisetsu" >}}): **2,551** facilities. Since the 2015 reforms, new admissions to special nursing homes have been focused on heavier need.

**Long-term care health facility ({{< ja "介護老人保健施設" "kaigo rōjin hoken shisetsu" >}}, {{< ja "老健" "rōken" >}}).** A rehabilitation and medical-oversight facility, meant as a bridge back home, though many stays are long. **4,214** facilities, **365,939** beds, occupancy **88.2 percent**. Numbers are drifting down.

**Long-term care medical centre ({{< ja "介護医療院" "kaigo iryō-in" >}}).** Created in the 2018 reform for people who need both daily care and ongoing medical care, including end of life. It replaced the old long-term care beds inside hospitals ({{< ja "介護療養型医療施設" "kaigo ryōyō-gata iryō shisetsu" >}}), which were abolished in March 2024. **917** centres, **52,837** beds, up 15.9 percent in facilities and 12.5 percent in beds on the year, occupancy **91.1 percent**.

**Group home ({{< ja "認知症対応型共同生活介護" "ninchi-shō taiō-gata kyōdō seikatsu kaigo" >}}).** Small shared houses for people with dementia, designated by the municipality. **14,341** offices.

**Specified facility ({{< ja "特定施設入居者生活介護" "tokutei shisetsu nyūkyo-sha seikatsu kaigo" >}}).** This is not a building type. It is a designation that lets a fee-charging home ({{< ja "有料老人ホーム" "yūryō rōjin hōmu" >}}) or similar residence bill insurance for the care delivered inside it. **5,969** such offices, up 1.7 percent. Many private senior residences are **not** specified facilities. Residents there buy care from home-visit or day providers under the ordinary monthly cap, or pay privately.

**Serviced senior housing ({{< ja "サービス付き高齢者向け住宅" "sābisu-tsuki kōreisha-muke jūtaku" >}}, {{< ja "サ高住" "sa-kō-jū" >}})** is a housing registration, mainly under housing law, with a staff presence. It is not automatically an insurance facility. This brief does **not** cite a national building count, because a current primary total was not opened and verified here. Treat サ高住 as housing-plus, and check in each case whether care is billed as {{< ja "特定施設" "tokutei shisetsu" >}} or as ordinary home services.

## Who runs them

Ownership is split by service, not by a single "public versus private" label. Shares below are from the detailed sample of the 1 October 2024 survey.[^1]

| Setting | Largest operator type |
| --- | --- |
| Special nursing home (30+ beds) | Social-welfare corporations other than social-welfare councils: **95.7 percent**. Companies are not in the table. |
| Community-based special nursing home | Social-welfare corporations: **99.0 percent** |
| Health facility ({{< ja "老健" "rōken" >}}) | Medical corporations ({{< ja "医療法人" "iryō hōjin" >}}): **75.5 percent**. Social-welfare corporations 16.1 percent. |
| Care medical centre ({{< ja "介護医療院" "kaigo iryō-in" >}}) | Medical corporations: **87.8 percent** |
| Home-visit care | Companies ({{< ja "営利法人" "eiri hōjin" >}}): **73.2 percent**. Social-welfare corporations 14.0 percent. |
| Day care ({{< ja "通所介護" "tsūsho kaigo" >}}) | Companies **55.0 percent**. Social-welfare corporations 34.0 percent. |
| Specified facilities (the insurance status used by many fee-charging homes) | Companies **69.6 percent**. Social-welfare corporations 21.1 percent. |
| Group homes | Companies **55.8 percent**. Social-welfare corporations 24.2 percent. |
| Care-manager offices | Companies **50.3 percent**. Social-welfare corporations 25.1 percent. Medical corporations 15.7 percent. |

A social-welfare corporation ({{< ja "社会福祉法人" "shakai fukushi hōjin" >}}) is a non-profit legal form. It is the gatekeeper of the special nursing home. A stock company can and does dominate home-visit care, day care, group homes, and fee-charging residences. Medical corporations dominate the facilities that sit next to hospitals. For a vendor, "public-sector nursing home" in the Thai sense maps most closely onto the special nursing home, and that buyer is a social-welfare corporation, not a ministry and not a private chain. The faster-moving, more numerous buyer is the company running visit care or day care.

Staffing on the same date, from the detailed tables: **299,123** care workers in special nursing homes, **124,137** in health facilities, **520,612** home-visit helpers, and **221,372** care staff in day care. These are headcounts in the survey, not full-time equivalents, and they are not the national "care worker" total used in workforce projections (that total was about **2.15 million** in fiscal 2022; see the budget post).

## There is no fixed budget per resident

Thai public homes often think in terms of a support budget allocated per resident. Japan does not work that way. Three different mechanisms get confused:

1. **Home and community services.** The person's care level sets a **monthly ceiling in units** (the caps in the previous post: 16,765 units for {{< ja "要介護" "yō-kaigo" >}}1 up to 36,217 for 要介護5, unchanged in the notice since 1 October 2019).[^3] The care manager spends that ceiling across visit care, day care, short stay, equipment, and so on. Unused units are not cash. They expire at month end. Units over the cap are private pay. This is the nearest thing to a "per person budget," and it belongs to the **user**, not to the facility.

2. **Designated facilities ({{< ja "特養" "toku-yō" >}}, {{< ja "老健" "rōken" >}}, {{< ja "介護医療院" "kaigo iryō-in" >}}).** Revenue is a **per-day fee** that depends on care level, room type, and staffing, plus add-ons. The July 2025 MHLW overview illustrates a unit-type private room in a special nursing home at 670 units a day for 要介護1 and 955 units a day for 要介護5.[^4] Multiply by the regional unit price (10.00 to 10.90 yen for these facilities) and by days in the month, then subtract the user's 10/20/30 percent and add whatever room and meal charges insurance does not cover. A full building's income is residents × days × that formula. There is no envelope handed to the director each April labelled "budget per bed."

3. **Municipal premiums and plans.** Every three years the municipality forecasts how much service it will pay for and sets the premium for people aged 65 and over so the books balance. That is a budget for the **insurer**, not a cap on an individual home. The national average premium for fiscal 2024–2026 is 6,225 yen a month.

Add-ons sit on top of both the home cap and the facility per-diem, and many of them are where technology policy shows up: report to LIFE, install sensors, improve wages, and the fee schedule pays extra units. Those rules are in the next post.

## Who supervises whom

Prefectures, designated cities, and core cities designate and supervise most home services and the three facility types. Municipalities designate and supervise community-based services (the small homes, group homes, neighbourhood day care, 24-hour visiting) and the care-manager offices. A product sold nationally still has to survive local designation rules, local unit prices, and a claim sent to that prefecture's federation.

Market size, in money rather than buildings: reviewed care costs in fiscal 2025 were **12.2208 trillion yen**.[^5] That is the flow through claims. It is not a count of private-pay fees in non-designated residences, and it is not a software market size. No official software-market figure was verified for this brief.
