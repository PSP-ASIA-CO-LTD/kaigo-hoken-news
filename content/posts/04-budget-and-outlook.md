The care system is already one of the largest public programmes in Japan, and the official view is that it gets harder from here: more people over 85, fewer workers, and a fee schedule that cannot rise as fast as wages in other industries.

## Three numbers that are not the same

Keep these apart. Mixing them is how market slides go wrong.

| What | Amount | When | What it includes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| National general-account line labelled {{< ja "介護" "kaigo" >}} | **3.7901 trillion yen** in the fiscal 2026 budget bill, up from 3.7374 trillion in the fiscal 2025 initial budget (＋1.4 percent) | Fiscal 2026 bill | The **national budget appropriation**, not total system spending.[^1] |
| Care benefit in the fiscal 2025 budget picture | **13.2 trillion yen** benefit, **14.3 trillion** on a total-cost basis | Fiscal 2025 budget, as printed in July 2025 | The financing diagram in the MHLW overview.[^2] Includes the premium and tax shares discussed in the insurance post. |
| Reviewed cost ({{< ja "費用額" "hiyō-gaku" >}}) | **12.2208 trillion yen**, ＋2.4 percent on fiscal 2024 | Fiscal 2025, published 30 September 2026 | Insurance payment plus public shares plus user co-payment. Excludes municipal direct payments such as equipment purchases and home modifications.[^3] |

An older series in the July 2025 overview, insurance benefit plus community-support projects and **excluding** user charges, is drawn through fiscal 2022 and labelled around **11.0 trillion yen** at the right-hand side of the chart. Use it only as history. The fiscal 2025 reviewed-cost figure is the one to quote for "how big the claim flow is now."

Inside the fiscal 2026 bill, the line "further development of community-based integrated care" is **235.7 billion yen**, against 241.7 billion the year before. The bill text says this covers the regional medical and long-term care fund and related support for building services and securing staff. It is not a software budget.

## The fiscal 2026 wage package

The same budget bill does not wait for the regular fee revision. It states a mid-cycle care-fee revision of **＋2.03 percent**, because the fiscal 2027 revision would otherwise come too late. The measures it lists:[^1]

- A broad wage rise for care workers, not only the staff already inside the older wage add-ons: **10,000 yen a month** (3.3 percent).
- A further **7,000 yen a month** (2.4 percent) for care staff at operators that take up productivity and collaboration measures.
- Together, and counting a **2,000 yen** regular annual increment, up to **19,000 yen a month** (6.3 percent) for care staff.
- A **100 yen a day** rise in the standard food cost used for facility benefits. For low-income users, the user's own food charge is either frozen or raised by 30 to 60 yen a day, depending on the income band.

Prefectural claim pages already carry service-code tables headed "Reiwa 8, June and August," so the mid-cycle change did not land as a single code drop.[^4] This brief does not pin the whole ＋2.03 percent on one effective date beyond what those tables show.

Wage add-ons are only useful if the software can calculate them and prove the office met the conditions. The fiscal 2024 revision had already merged several older wage add-ons into {{< ja "介護職員等処遇改善加算" "kaigo shokuin tō shogū kaizen kasan" >}}. Offices that cannot document the rules lose the units. Tokyo Shoko Research, writing on 9 January 2026 about calendar-2025 bankruptcies, said plainly that small operators often fail to use the productivity-linked wage add-on.[^5] That is a software and operations problem, not only a wage problem.

## What fiscal 2027 is expected to decide

The regular revision is fiscal 2027, lined up with the 10th municipal plan period (fiscal 2027–2029). MHLW's July 2025 overview, citing the care-insurance subcommittee of December 2024, says system changes are being aimed at that plan so that premiums do not jump in the middle of a three-year cycle.[^2]

Still open in the December 2023 cabinet reform roadmap, as reprinted in that overview:

- How far to extend the **20 percent user charge**, with a conclusion sought before fiscal 2027. Not decided in the sources used here.
- What to do about care-management benefits and about daily-living assistance for people with lighter need, with a conclusion sought by the start of the 10th plan.
- Staffing-ratio exceptions where technology has been shown to work, possibly before a full fee revision if the evidence is accepted.
- Room charges for multi-bed rooms. The roadmap said some facilities would start a room charge of about **8,000 yen a month** from August 2025. This brief did not re-check a later notice to confirm how that was implemented.

Home-visit care's base fee is a known sore point going into 2027. Industry coverage in 2026 describes providers asking for a higher base rate while the finance ministry looks at cases where many users live in one building and the visit is cheaper to deliver. That debate is not restated here as a decided cut or a decided rise. The decided 2026 action is the mid-cycle ＋2.03 percent package above, which is a wage and food-cost measure, not a rewrite of the visit-care base table.

## People: the constraint that drives the software agenda

MHLW summed the care-worker numbers that prefectures built from the 9th municipal plans:[^6]

- About **2.15 million** care workers in fiscal 2022 (from the 2022 facility survey).
- About **2.40 million** needed in fiscal 2026 (about 250,000 more, roughly 63,000 a year).
- About **2.72 million** needed in fiscal 2040 (about 570,000 more than 2022, roughly 32,000 a year).

The notes to that paper limit the count to care staff in insured services, plus the part of the municipal prevention programme that replaced the old preventive visit and day services. It is not every person who works in a senior residence.

The July 2025 overview, using the 2023 population projection from the National Institute of Population and Social Security Research, puts Japan's total population at **112.84 million in 2040**, down from 126.15 million in 2020, and on a path to under 90 million and an ageing rate of **38.7 percent by 2070**.[^2] The same deck says the certification rate rises sharply with age: about **19.5 percent** of everyone 65 and over, **30.8 percent** of everyone 75 and over, and **58.2 percent** of everyone 85 and over. The slide cites both September 2023 and September 2024 certification files, so treat those rates as the deck's figures rather than as a single cleaned table. The point MHLW draws is that 85-and-over is the group that drives need, and that group was still growing.

Demand does not peak in the same year everywhere. The overview's insurer-level projection, built from late-2022 use rates and the 2023 regional population forecast, shows many urban insurers still rising past 2040, while some insurers have already peaked. A product strategy that assumes "Japan ageing means every town needs more beds" is wrong. Cities need more capacity, especially 24-hour home care. Depopulating towns need services that can survive with fewer staff and fewer users.

## Distress on the ground, calendar 2025

Tokyo Shoko Research, 9 January 2026, counted **176** bankruptcies in elderly-welfare and care businesses in calendar 2025, with liabilities of at least 10 million yen. That was a record for a second year, and about 60 percent above the 111 in 2019. **91** were home-visit care businesses, also a record for a third year. Day and short-stay businesses were 45, fee-charging homes 16, and dementia group homes 9. Sales failure was the stated cause in 140 cases. Most failed firms had fewer than 10 employees. The research firm tied the visit-care cluster to the negative base-fee change, helper shortages, and fuel costs, and it warned that 2026 could look similar.[^5]

This is the public mood that matters commercially, more than a social-media index this brief did not measure. Families worry about whether a helper will come. Workers compare care wages with other jobs. Owners of small visit-care offices are closing. Large social-welfare corporations that run special nursing homes are more stable (occupancy was 94.5 percent in the 2024 survey) but cannot hire. The political argument is about who pays: higher premiums, a wider 20 percent user charge, or a larger tax share. None of those choices shrinks the need for fewer hours of paperwork per resident.

## What to watch next

- Whether the fiscal 2027 fee revision raises home-visit base rates or mostly extends wage and productivity add-ons.
- The decision, if it comes before fiscal 2027, on how many users move from 10 percent to 20 percent co-payment. That changes what billing software must explain to families.
- How many municipalities actually switch on the care information platform between now and April 2028. A national go-live date is not the same as a national go-live.
- LIFE's next simplification. The September 2026 committee paper treats overlapping items across 17 add-ons as an open problem. Vendors who hard-code today's forms will rebuild them.
- Bankruptcies and office closures in visit care through 2026. If the small-office segment keeps shrinking, the buyer is a larger corporation with many sites, and the product has to run a headquarters view, not one site at a time.
